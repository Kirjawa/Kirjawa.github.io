    How far three spots go

    Each bar is the share of a kindergarten's families reached as spots are added, best first. By car, a few junctions go a long way, because every route funnels into the same roads. Stops and everyday places each reach a smaller slice, so they work as a set.

    Car: families passing the junction on the way in. Public transport: families boarding at the stop or living beside it. Places: children living within a 10-minute walk. Shares are of each kindergarten's own catchment.

    How it was made: the question, data, method, checks and limits

    1The question

    A kindergarten draws its children from a small area, and parents choose it for being near home or on the way. So an advertisement works where those parents already are every day: on the roads they drive, at the stop where they wait, at the pharmacy, the playground and the supermarket. The question is which of those places reach the most families who could realistically bring a child to each of the five.

    These are private kindergartens. Families travel further for them than for a municipal one, and more of them drive, so the roads matter as much as the metro. And an advertisement isn't always a billboard: a stack of leaflets at a children's clinic can reach the right parents for far less.

    Three kinds of spot are ranked for each kindergarten, and kept apart, because a billboard, a shelter poster and a leaflet are different purchases. A fourth list shows the spots that serve two or more kindergartens at once.

    3Method in brief

    1. Where children live. The 2021 census gives residents under 15 for each square kilometre. Each cell's children are spread over 100 m pixels in proportion to the residents the Joint Research Centre's population grid puts there. Under 15 is the youngest group the census grid publishes; it stands in for families with children of kindergarten age.
    2. How close is close. A family counts in full when the kindergarten is up to 5 minutes away by car, and less and less up to 20 minutes, where it counts for nothing. By public transport the same runs from 10 to 30 minutes, door to door. These are assumptions, written down before the results; one answer from the kindergartens about where their families live would replace them.
    3. By car. For the morning rush hour, speeds are set at 55% of each road's limit, with 20 seconds lost at every traffic signal. Each pixel's fastest route to the kindergarten is added up on the road segments it uses, in the direction of travel. Signals within 60 m of each other are one junction. Junctions are then picked one at a time: each next one is the junction that adds the most families not already passing an earlier pick. Junctions within 300 m of the door are left to the kindergarten's own sign.
    4. By public transport. From the timetable of Wednesday 18 November 2026, 07:00 to 09:00: the time from every stop to the door, with the wait (half the interval between vehicles), the ride, at most one change, and the walk from the last stop. Each family takes the stop that gets it there fastest, within 600 m of home. Where walking all the way is faster, the family is counted at its local metro station or stop, because that is where it would see a poster.
    5. Where parents already go. Seven kinds of place from OpenStreetMap: medical centres, pharmacies, playgrounds, children's shops, sports, dance, music and language schools, supermarkets and shopping centres. A place's reach is the children living within a 10-minute walk of it, weighted by how close they are to the kindergarten. Places are picked like junctions, each adding the most children not already near an earlier pick, and no more than two of a kind.
    6. Shared spots. A junction, stop or place is shared when it reaches at least 5%, 1.5% or 3% of the catchments of two or more kindergartens.
    7. Reach, and the neighbourhoods inside it. The slider draws the area within 15 minutes to an hour of each kindergarten. By car, that is along the road network at rush-hour speeds. By public transport, the time is split in two: 10 minutes on foot to a stop (550 m in a straight line), and the rest from that stop to the door, which covers the wait, the ride, one change at most and the walk at the other end. So at 20 minutes the map highlights the stops within 10 minutes of the door and shades everything within a 10-minute walk of them. The list to download holds every district, neighbourhood and village named in OpenStreetMap whose centre falls inside. The ranked spots don't move with the slider: they keep the reach of step 2.

    4By car

    For every kindergarten the families' routes funnel into a few roads before they reach the door, so the first junctions on those roads are passed by a large share of the catchment. At Lozenets, Simeonovsko Shose and Boyana three junctions are passed by 86% or more. At Ovcha Kupel and Lyulin families arrive from more directions, and the same three reach 63% and 54%: more junctions, or the other two channels, do the rest. 11 junctions are on the way to two kindergartens; the "All five" view lists the best of them.

    5By public transport

    At Lozenets and Lyulin the metro does the work. The kindergarten's own station comes first, because the families within walking distance live around it and pass it every day, and the next stations along the line follow. At Simeonovsko Shose, Boyana and Ovcha Kupel the first stop is a bus, tram or trolleybus stop, and the families are spread thinly: a few per cent at each stop. Here a poster inside the vehicles of one line would reach more than a poster at any one shelter.

    There is no count to check the routes against: Sofia publishes no traffic counts by street and no passengers by stop. The ranking shows where the catchment's families are likely to pass, in order. It is not a measured audience.

    8What open data can't tell here

    • Whether a spot can be bought. No public register lists billboard sites, shelter posters or notice boards. The lists say where to look; a media agency or a walk down the street says what is there.
    • Where the kindergartens' families really come from, and how they arrive. The catchment sizes are assumptions. The enrolment list, by neighbourhood, would replace them.
    • Who can afford the fees. The census grid has no income. Every family with children counts the same here.
    • Traffic and passengers. Routes are modelled from speed limits and the timetable. A junction can carry many cars that have nothing to do with these families, which is what a billboard there is priced on.
    • Daily routes other than the way to the kindergarten: the commute, the school run of an older child.
    • Children of kindergarten age, as opposed to all under 15, and anything that changed since the 2021 census, such as new housing.
    • Small businesses missing from OpenStreetMap. Pharmacies and supermarkets are well mapped; private clinics and children's activity centres less so.
    • Visibility: trees, parked lorries, which side of the junction has a free wall.
    • Neighbourhood boundaries. OpenStreetMap has a point for most neighbourhoods in Sofia, not an outline, so a neighbourhood is in or out of the reach by its centre.
    • Trips with two changes, which the public transport reach leaves out.

    9What the checks caught

    The first ranking of junctions put three in a row on the same boulevard at the top: the same families, counted three times. Picking each next junction for the families it adds fixed that. The first public-transport ranking left each kindergarten's own metro station with no families at all, because everyone living near it walks; they are now counted at the station they live beside. One junction came out as the ring road crossing itself, because the ring road carries two names in the south-west. And the road network first came from a server that turned out to be four months behind, which only the file's own date showed; it was rebuilt from the November extract, and the picks stayed the same.

    The scripts were written with an AI agent and checked as described here; how I work with AI on GIS.
