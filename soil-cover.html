Dobrudja: how long does the soil lie bare?

Bare soil loses carbon, dries out and blows away. Since 2023, EU farm payments come with a soil-cover rule, and in Bulgaria it asks farmers to keep at least 80% of their arable land covered from 1 June to 30 September. Stubble counts as cover. So I asked: how many days a year does each field actually lie bare, and did anything change after the rule?

I looked at Dobrudja, Bulgaria's grain belt, where wheat, sunflower and maize cover almost 500,000 ha. This page explains how I measured it, what I found, and where the method stops.

Results out

In more detail:

  1. Fields and crops. Bulgaria publishes its farm parcel register every year, with the crop declared on each field. I used every year from 2018 to 2025.
  2. A sample, not the whole region. Instead of all of Dobrich and Silistra provinces, I measured 8 areas of 10 × 10 km spread from the Danube to the Black Sea coast. That is about 10,000 fields and 72,500 ha a year, roughly 15% of the region's farmland. Results are weighted back to the region's crop mix.
  3. Every clear satellite image. Each field was measured on every Sentinel-2 image where it was clear of cloud and shadow, about 64 images per field per year. Snow is kept separate, using the snow class in the images and NASA's daily MODIS snow map to fill cloudy days.
  4. A state for every day. Each day, every field is one of: living plants, stubble or crop residue, bare soil, snow, or unknown when the nearest clear image is more than 20 days away. The states come from greenness (NDVI), near infrared and a tillage index that picks up dry plant residue (NDTI).
  5. Thresholds set on cases where the answer is known. There are no field visits, so I used the crop calendar: rapeseed is green all winter, spring seedbeds are bare in April, wheat stubble lies on the field right after harvest, and wet soil after rain or snow is bare. The thresholds that separate these groups best are 89% accurate for living plants and 97% for stubble against bare soil.
  6. Weather and water. Daily temperature and rain for each field (ERA5-Land and ERA5), and Sentinel-1 radar for standing water after heavy rain.
  7. Testing the rule. Each field is compared with itself before and after 2023, taking out the crop, the previous crop and spring rain. The uncertainty comes from the 7 yearly values, not from 70,000 fields, so it stays honest.

Fewer fields sit bare all summer, but the change came before the rule

In 2019, 8% of the farmland was bare for most of the summer, and in the dry year 2020 it was 20%. Almost all of it was wheat, barley and rapeseed after harvest: fields tilled soon after harvest and left open until autumn sowing, or stubble so thin after the drought that it reads as bare soil. From 2021 on it has stayed between 1.5% and 5%. That drop happened two years before the rule. When I compare the same fields with themselves and take out the weather, the years after 2023 are no different: −2 ± 16 days in summer. Weather moves these numbers far more than the rule does.

The crop matters more than the place

Wheat and barley are harvested in July and their stubble counts as cover, so they have about 60 to 90 bare days a year. Sunflower and maize leave the soil open from autumn to late spring, about 160 to 200 days. Between areas the differences are smaller. The two areas by the Danube are the least bare and one central area is the barest, but there is no clean pattern from the Danube to the coast.

Winter is the gap

Since August 2024 the Bulgarian rule covers summer only. Winter is when the soil is most exposed: before sunflower and maize there is no living cover on most of the 120 days from November to February. Telling stubble from bare soil in winter is harder, because wet soil makes bare ground look like stubble. So I only made that call on days when the topsoil was dry. On those days it was mostly bare soil: stubble made up only 5 to 27% of them, depending on the year.

2025 was greener in winter, but mostly not from cover crops

In winter 2024 to 2025, 15% of the land waiting for sunflower or maize was green for most of the winter, against 1 to 4% in every earlier year. It followed by far the wettest late summer of the period. On the images, a few of these fields look sown, as a cover crop would be, but most look like weeds and cereal regrowth. The register only records the main crop, so satellite data alone can't prove a cover crop.

How I checked it

  • A random spot-check. In the pilot area I drew 15 random field dates and judged each one on the image. About 11 to 13 of 15 were right, and the misses were borderline cases.
  • 200 views, labelled blind. I drew 200 random field views across all areas and years and labelled each image by eye without seeing what the method had called it. Of the views that were clear enough to judge, living plants were right 89% of the time, while stubble and bare soil were right about 65 to 70% of the time. They get confused with each other in both directions, by about the same amount, so the yearly shares stay fair, but a single field on a single day is less certain. On the views taken on wet soil that the method no longer calls, 15 of 16 were bare soil, which supports leaving them out.
  • The two winter signals, on the images. 12 random fields for each. The green winter of 2025 held up, although much of it is patchy. The 2019 "stubble" did not: all 12 were dark, wet, tilled soil.
  • Wet soil, measured. Published work warns that the stubble index is thrown off by soil moisture. With daily topsoil moisture from ERA5-Land, the effect is clear here too: on sunflower and maize land in winter and at sowing, 1 to 6% of views read as stubble when the topsoil is dry, but 44 to 69% when it is very wet. So in winter, stubble and bare soil are only told apart on dry days, and the rest is shown as "too wet to tell". The odd 2019 winter falls back in line.
  • Floods. Dark radar after rain also comes from wheat in ear, not only from water. Fields flagged in wheat during heading were dropped, and so were faulty radar scenes from 2017 to 2018. What remains is under 1% of the farmland in any year.
  • Distance to water, tested and dropped. Fields near rivers and lakes first looked barer. Inside each area the effect changed direction from one area to the next, so it came from where the areas are, not from the water.

What this can't tell you

This measures how long the soil lies bare, field by field. It can't say whether a farm meets the rule: the rule applies to 80% of each holding, and the public register doesn't say which fields belong to the same farm. No farm is named or can be identified here.

  • These are sample estimates from 8 areas, weighted to the region. Patterns between areas are described, not tested.
  • In winter the soil is often too wet to tell stubble from bare soil. Those days are shown separately, not guessed.
  • The register records the main crop only, so green land between crops may be a cover crop, weeds or regrowth.
  • The rule changed in August 2024, so 2024 is a transition year.
  • Weather data is about 9 km across. It describes the local climate, not each field.

Going to the whole region, or a country

The same steps run wall to wall. On a laptop, the whole region would take about 16 to 20 hours of processing. Running it in the cloud next to the imagery, or server-side on the Copernicus Data Space with openEO, brings that down to about an hour. A new region needs its own threshold check and spot-check before its numbers can be trusted.
