Desk-based habitat pre-map and monitoring for biodiversity net gain

Demonstration on open data. Not a field survey and not signed off by an ecologist. A satellite pre-map is an input for the ecologist, never a survey result.

Fenwick Solar Farm, north of Doncaster, was consented in February 2026. Before the application, ecologists surveyed it from February 2023 to August 2024 for its biodiversity net gain assessment, and published the baseline: about 71% cropland and 26% grassland, with 28 km of hedgerows.

How much of that could open satellite data and LiDAR have anticipated before anyone went on site? And what can the record of past years, floods and weather add that a single survey can't?

The pre-map against the published survey. Select the image to open it full size.

  • Broad habitats: 75% cropland and 20% grassland, against 71% and 26% in the survey. Another 3.5% is marked unclear, for the walkover to look at first.
  • A second opinion: the Crop Map of England gives the same answer, cropland or grassland, on 93.3% of the area.
  • Hedgerows from LiDAR: 17 km inside the site and 29 km with the boundary hedges, against 28 km in the survey.
  • The past: about 19 ha that had been grass for at least three years were ploughed in September 2023, inside the survey period. The Crop Map of England shows grass there in 2021, 2022 and 2023, and cereals in 2024. Under the metric, land degraded after 30 January 2020 takes its earlier habitat as the baseline, so these are the first fields to check.
  • Water: ten winters of radar, checked against Sentinel-2, put open water on about 5 ha inside the site in at least three winters, mostly along the River Went, and the amount follows winter rainfall.
  • Ready for the metric: 142 parcels in the order of the statutory metric's baseline inputs, with areas that add up exactly to the site.

1The question

A biodiversity net gain baseline starts with a habitat map, and for a large site it takes weeks of survey. A desk-based pre-map can show the ecologist where the common habitats are, where to look first, and what has changed on the land before the survey date, which matters: where habitat was degraded after 30 January 2020, the baseline has to be the habitat before the change.

Fenwick was chosen because its baseline is published, recent and made with the statutory metric. Biodiversity net gain becomes mandatory for nationally significant infrastructure projects on 2 November 2026.

For the monitoring side, on a solar park that is already built, see the Cleve Hill sample.

3Method in brief

  1. Site. The solar site, which the survey covered in full, is the main block of the published Order limits: 406.6 ha, against about 407 ha in the assessment.
  2. Pre-map. Fields are found as areas of similar seasonal greenness, with LiDAR hedgerows as breaks. Each field is classed from its own record over the survey period: cropland when it shows bare soil in at least two months, grassland when it stays green, woodland where LiDAR finds tall vegetation in blocks, otherwise unclear. The rules were written before the comparison and not tuned to it.
  3. Hedgerows and trees. From LiDAR: woody vegetation more than 0.8 m tall that is not a roof, narrower than about 20 m, measured along its centre line in runs of at least 20 m.
  4. History. The same rules, farming year by farming year from 2017/18, with drought summers taken into account: in a summer much drier than usual, browned grass can look like bare soil.
  5. Water. Every winter radar pass: open water where both radar polarisations are much darker than that orbit's usual winter. Passes with frost or snow are skipped, since both darken radar just as water does. Where Sentinel-2 had a clear view within two days, it acts as the referee: radar water that looks like bare soil or green cover there is removed.
  6. Table. Parcels cut along the hedgerows, laid out in the order of the metric's baseline inputs: broad habitat and area filled in, habitat type suggested, condition left to the survey.

5What satellite data can't tell here

  • Condition. It needs the field survey and the condition criteria. The pre-map gives broad type only.
  • Finer types. Other neutral grassland and modified grassland look alike from space; so do cereals and some other crops at this level.
  • Small and narrow habitats. Ditches, tracks, yards and grass margins under 10 m wide: in the survey they add up to about 3% of the site.
  • Species, and anything that depends on them.
  • The date of the LiDAR. The composite mixes survey years, so a hedge planted or removed since then is not reflected.
  • Water under crops or trees, and flooding that drained between two radar passes.

6How AI was used

An agent (Claude Code) wrote the scripts: downloads from the open services, the pre-map and parcels, the LiDAR measurements, the flood checks, the figures and the table. Every result on this page was checked against something independent: the published survey totals, the Crop Map of England, and the Environment Agency's flood outlines.

What it got wrong, and what caught it: the first hedgerow measurement treated the trees in the hedges as woodland and found 8 km; the survey's 28 km showed something was missing. The first winter water record didn't follow the weather: the dry winter 2018/19 came out wettest. Sentinel-2 views taken within two days of the radar showed that 83% of what radar called water was green crops and grass. With both radar polarisations and Sentinel-2 as the referee, 82% of the radar's water is now confirmed as water. And the first crop map download covered only part of the site, until a gap in the history check showed it.
